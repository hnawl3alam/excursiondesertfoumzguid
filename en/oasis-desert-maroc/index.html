What exactly is an oasis in the Moroccan desert?

Forget the cartoon pool ringed by three palm trees. In southern Morocco, an oasis is first and foremost farmland: a place where people have found water, at the foot of a mountain, along a seasonal river or above an aquifer, and have worked the land around it for generations. Without that work, the oasis would simply not exist.

A traditional palm grove is farmed in three layers:

  • the canopy: date palms, giving fruit and shade;
  • the middle layer: fruit trees such as pomegranates;
  • the ground layer, in the shade: vegetables, fodder and cereals.

Stacking crops like this keeps the ground cool and moist and gets a lot out of a small area. Around the gardens you will find earthen fortified villages (ksour) and, here and there, kasbahs. So when you visit one of Morocco’s desert oases, you are walking through someone’s farm and village, not a film set.

Where the water comes from: rivers, aquifers and khettaras

Everything hinges on water. Depending on the place, it comes from a river that runs for part of the year, a spring or groundwater. To get it to the gardens, Moroccan oasis farmers developed the khettara (also spelt khattara or rhettara).

The idea is simple and clever. A tunnel is dug from the water table at a gentler gradient than the land above it, so the water eventually emerges at the surface by gravity alone. A line of vertical shafts at regular intervals is used to dig and maintain the tunnel. It belongs to the same family as the Iranian qanat and the Algerian foggara.

  • Khettaras are found in the Drâa-Tafilalet and Marrakech regions, among others.
  • Near Tata, the oasis of Agadir Lehna has a khettara and a water clock (clepsydra) you can see.
  • Over the last thirty years or so, many khettaras have run into trouble and a good number have been abandoned.

Foum Zguid’s palm grove and the oases of the Jebel Bani

Foum Zguid is an oasis in its own right, sitting below the Jebel Bani, the rocky ridge that runs across Tata Province. A stroll along the shaded paths of its palm grove is the easiest way to understand how an oasis works before you head out into the desert.

  • Allougoum, about 25 km north, is another Jebel Bani oasis, with an earthen ksar being restored: mosque, houses and covered lanes.
  • Zaouiat Sidi Abdenabi, to the south, is a traditional village at the foot of the ridge with a marabout’s tomb. Our trips stop here for lunch in an earthen house.

For the town itself, its Thursday market and practical tips, read our Foum Zguid travel guide.

Tissint: the oasis with salt-water waterfalls

About an hour’s drive from Foum Zguid towards Tata (and roughly 70 km from Tata itself), Tissint is the most unusual oasis in the area. Its name is said to mean “salt” in Amazigh, and in caravan days it was known for its highly prized salty water. Today people come for the cascades, where you can walk along the banks, cool off and swim in natural pools.

There is more to Tissint than the falls:

  • a palm grove and a small medina;
  • the house where the explorer Charles de Foucauld stayed for several weeks, in the old Jewish quarter;
  • a name known to planetary scientists: a meteorite from Mars that landed near the town in July 2011 is called the Tissint meteorite.

Our Tissint waterfalls day trip from Foum Zguid (from €100) adds tea in a nearby Berber village, a short walk into a canyon and a riverside lunch where you see tafernoute, a traditional bread, being made.

Laarjimia and El Grisim: remote oases in the Foum Zguid desert

Further south, towards Iriki National Park, the oases become smaller and more remote. You reach them only by 4×4 or on foot.

Laarjimia oasis

It is around 25 km of track from Foum Zguid to Laarjimia, through ochre hills and the flat-topped mountains of the Iriki park, including a summit of Djbel Madouar El Kbir shaped like a tagine. Once there, you wander among the date palms, have a wood-fired lunch in the shade and, if you like, climb a nearby hill for the view. That is the Laarjimia oasis excursion (from €90).

El Grisim oasis

El Grisim is a stage on our treks between the dunes of Erg El Ghoule and Erg Chigaga. You walk in with the camel caravan and spend the night under the palms. The programme includes an introduction to how oases work, bread baked in the sand and the tea ritual. It features on the Oasis to Erg Chigaga trek (6 days, from €650) and the Trek in the heart of the dunes (5 days, from €650).

The Drâa valley: Morocco’s great oasis route

The Drâa is Morocco’s longest river, at around 1,100 km. It is formed where the Dadès and Imini rivers meet, and since 1971 its flow has been regulated by the El Mansour Eddahbi dam. Below Tagounite it is dry for most of the year.

Its upper valley, roughly 200 km long, strings together six stretches of palm groves from the Mezguita oasis near Agdz down to M’Hamid El Ghizlane, dotted with ksour. It is one of the best-known oasis routes in the country, and Iriki National Park, south of Foum Zguid, reaches as far as the dry bed of the Drâa.

Two of our tours pair the Drâa valley with the desert: Ouarzazate to Erg Chigaga via Foum Zguid (from €300), through Zagora, Tamegroute and M’Hamid, and the Zagora – Erg Chigaga – Foum Zguid circuit (from €235). Both return across Lake Iriki.

Fragile places: how to visit an oasis responsibly

Morocco’s desert oases are under strain. Around Iriki National Park, the regional tourism board reports that dune cover has grown by about 20% in fifty years, threatening palm groves and irrigation channels. Drought and expanding farmland put pressure on water reserves that are often fossil, and bayoud, a fungal disease of the date palm first recorded in Morocco in 1870, remains a threat.

A few habits make a real difference:

  • Stick to the paths: every garden belongs to a family and every channel has its turn of water.
  • Do not pick dates or fruit unless you are invited to.
  • Always ask before photographing people, especially women and children.
  • Go easy on water in camps and guesthouses; it often has to be brought from a distance.
  • Cover shoulders and knees in villages.
  • Spend locally: dates, cooperative crafts, meals with families.
  • Leave nothing behind, not even food scraps.

Frequently asked questions

What is an oasis in the Moroccan desert?

It is an irrigated, farmed area in an arid landscape, created by people where water can be reached: a river, a spring or an aquifer. A traditional palm grove has three layers: date palms on top, fruit trees in the middle, and vegetables and cereals on the ground. Earthen villages, or ksour, cluster around the gardens.

What is a khettara?

A khettara is a traditional irrigation system. An underground tunnel runs from the water table at a gentler slope than the ground, so the water reaches the surface by gravity. Vertical shafts along its length are used for maintenance. Khettaras exist in the Drâa-Tafilalet and Marrakech regions, and one can be seen at Agadir Lehna near Tata.

Which oases can I visit near Foum Zguid?

Start with Foum Zguid’s own palm grove. Then there is Tissint with its salt-water cascades (about an hour towards Tata), Laarjimia oasis (about 25 km of track), Allougoum and its ksar about 25 km north, and Zaouiat Sidi Abdenabi below the Jebel Bani. El Grisim oasis is reached on foot during our treks.

Can you swim at the Tissint waterfalls?

Yes. Visitors walk along the banks, cool off and swim in the natural pools. The water is salty, which is what made the oasis famous with the old caravans. Bring modest swimwear and sandals, and ask about the flow before you go, as it changes with the season and recent rainfall.

How much is an oasis trip from Foum Zguid?

The Laarjimia oasis day trip starts from €90 per person and the Tissint waterfalls day trip from €100. The two-day Foum Zguid desert trip, with lunch at Laajamiya oasis, starts from €130, and treks that overnight at El Grisim oasis start from €650. We confirm the final price before you book.

When is the best time to visit Morocco’s desert oases?

October to April, when days are mild. In summer temperatures often exceed 40 °C, so visits are best early in the morning or late in the day. Winter nights can be cold, particularly if you are sleeping out in the desert, and sandstorms can blow up in spring.

Are Morocco’s oases under threat?

Yes. Drought, falling water tables, abandoned khettaras and advancing sand all take their toll. Around Iriki National Park, the regional tourism board reports dune cover up by about 20% in fifty years, threatening palm groves and channels. Bayoud, a fungal disease of the date palm, has been present in Morocco since the 19th century.
